1 · What we found, in one paragraph

On a four-GPU Blackwell box, four independent copies of a mid-size model beat one large model spread across all four by several times, because replicas exchange nothing between GPUs and because weights compete with KV cache for the same 384 GB. Getting the best out of that configuration depended almost entirely on things set wrong on our side rather than on the hardware: KV cache dtype, the memory ceiling, whether the load generator actually saturated the box, and above all which kernel the engine silently chose. On 3 September that last point repeated itself on a second model in the opposite direction: the NVFP4 build of Qwen3.8-27B was 47% slower than FP8 until the sm_120 W4A4 kernel was forced, after which it was 64% faster. The hardware performs; the software picks badly by default; and the highest-intelligence open model that fits (GLM-5.3-Flash) now serves correctly here after a day of kernel work. The open question remains whether Scan will sell hardware we can configure this way.

The NVFP4 checkpoint carries 4-bit activation scales, so a true W4A4 tensor-core path exists, and vLLM ships two of them for capability 12 (b12x, flashinfer_b12x). Its auto-selection nevertheless took a weight-only W4A16 kernel that dequantises to 16-bit, which is bandwidth-friendly at batch 1 and compute-bound at ours: it barely moved from C256 to C1024 while FP8 climbed 19%. The published "+36% for NVFP4" figure understates what we see once the right kernel runs. Lesson, now twice-learned on this hardware: read the Using … Kernel line in the log and force the backend; never trust the default.

3 · GLM-5.3-Flash: the index-46 model now serves correctly

A hard-coded kernel choice is worse than a bad one: it stops the model loading at all. Overnight, eleven of about thirty arms died at engine start, and none of the failures was a property of the model. The FlashInfer CUTLASS mixture-of-experts backend, correct for gpt-oss and for three of the models above, is rejected outright by any four-bit checkpoint whose expert scales are grouped sixteen wide, which is how Ling-3.0-flash, MiMo-V2.5, Nemotron-3.5-Lightning and Qwen3.6-35B are built, and it rejects Step-3.7-Flash for its activation function. Forcing the fast four-bit linear kernel on gemma-4-26B asks for a tile the compiler does not have. A fixed sequence budget overruns a hybrid model's recurrent-state cache, and the memory ceiling that is right for a 96 GB card fails during graph capture on a 32 GB one. Every one of those is a launch flag. The campaign now runs a sweep harness that walks candidate kernel pairs per model, keeps and benchmarks each pair that serves, derives memory and sequence budgets from the card, and resumes where it left off. That is the fix and the per-model kernel sweep at once.

5 · Quality, measured properly

Throughput without a quality gate is worthless, and string comparison of greedy generations is not a gate: one differing token cascades, and two identical servers on this stack disagree on 3.8% of top-1 tokens anyway. Three instruments, each read against its own control — a corruption tripwire on every configuration before it is benchmarked, logit-level divergence for questions about a single serving flag, and a task suite for questions about a model.

The published intelligence index does predict this. GLM-5.3-Flash at index 46 leads, and leads on the families that separate models rather than saturate: maths, knowledge, long context. An earlier version of this page claimed the opposite. That claim was an artefact of a broken harness — no reasoning parser, a token cap that scored truncation as wrong, and house sampling instead of each vendor's — and is withdrawn; the same Qwen weights went from 0.558 to 0.732 when only the serving changed. What survives is narrower and more useful: gpt-oss is disproportionately good at code and tool calling for its size, and index 16 buys 0.933 on code where index 46 buys 0.864. Kimi K3, the other model named as a candidate, is 2.78 trillion parameters: it does not fit 384 GB at any precision and belongs to the 7-GPU node or an API.

The same suite against the API

Does the endpoint a customer buys at list price score what the weights score here? The 403 items run against OpenRouter too — default routing, the vendor's sampling recipe, the same caps, from the third box beside the GPU work; the key lives on the box. First result: GLM-5.3-Flash via OpenRouter scores 0.824 against 0.794 for our NVFP4 build at TP4 (0.809 with its MTP head). The gap sits where the quantisation ladder said quantisation lands — maths 0.812 vs 0.738, code 0.813 vs 0.760, knowledge 0.657 vs 0.600 — while truncation (6.2%) and answer length match ours. Z.AI serves the model at training precision; we serve a post-training NVFP4 build, and the native-FP8 GLM run queued on the original box is the direct test. DeepSeek-V4-Flash, Qwen3.8-27B and the one-card models follow.

Speculation is lossless — measured on paired items

GLM scored 0.800 without its MTP head and 0.740 with it on the 400 W box, and the previous version of this page called that a speculator bug. It was not. Compared on the 367 items both arms actually scored, same box, recipe and seed: no speculation 0.872, MTP 0.864; items only one side got right, 13 against 10 — a coin toss. The gap came from arms scored on different item sets under a cap that marked truncation wrong, and the MTP arm, being faster, was the one that finished the 36 items the base arm timed out on. Speculation did not cost GLM anything; it bought the hardest items within the same wall-clock. A greedy-sequence test could not have settled this: its own control, the same server captured twice, matched on only 4 of 12.

The second model says the same thing from the other side. DeepSeek-V4-Flash with NVIDIA's DSpark drafter (7 draft tokens a step, 37% accepted) scored 0.831 against 0.801 without it (the same layout on the Marlin expert kernel scored 0.814 without a drafter, and the DP4 + EP layout 0.844 — the headline no longer needs speculation), all 403 items both times, paired 19 to 7 in the drafter's favour. A drafter cannot make the model smarter — probabilistic drafting with rejection sampling preserves the target distribution by construction — so a 19-to-7 split at temperature 1.0 is a reading of the suite's own run-to-run noise at vendor sampling. The noise has a measured value.

Lossless is not free at saturation. GLM's MTP head at 1,024 concurrent streams: 592 output tokens a second against 911 without it, with only 10% of drafted tokens accepted at that batch (67% at low concurrency). DeepSeek's DSpark drafter: 665 against 1,107. A drafter buys per-request latency when the box is lightly loaded and sells aggregate throughput when it is full; for batch and agent-harness traffic on this node, run without it.

On throughput the frontier has two points: the gittensor build and FP8. On quality RedHat's four-bit build is at parity with FP8 (0.801 against 0.802), while the build every throughput number here was measured on is the weakest of the three, and that pair is a real difference (29 items only FP8 got right against 14, paired). The remaining cost is concentrated in mathematics — 0.076 even for the parity build, 0.153 for the fast one — with code, tools, long context and instruction following within noise. A caveat the numbers raise themselves: at Qwen's own sampling a maths answer averages 9,300 output tokens for FP8, 12,100 for RedHat, 13,900 for gittensor and 14,300 for the native BF16 parent (0.806 overall, 0.800 on maths, no timeouts), with 3%, 26%, 35% and 19% of items hitting the 32,768 cap and scoring as wrong. FP8's low truncation is partly the request timeout having removed its longest items, so its maths lead will shrink when they are completed; and four-bit PTQ makes the model reason longer without converging, which is a real cost that a 32k cap is too short to measure fully. The ladder is completed at these caps for comparability and re-run with 65k tokens of room. So the decisive question is whether RedHat's build loads under the W4A4 kernel: if it does, it is FP8 accuracy at 5,000 tokens a second, and that run is queued, as is the quantisation-aware-trained rung that could collapse the trade-off entirely.

Third-party post-training four-bit is the expensive one: 30 against 6, maths 0.662 against 0.800, 35% of maths answers at the cap against 19% for the parent. It is the build every throughput headline here was taken on, and it ships a rewritten chat template, so an arm serving its weights under Qwen's own template is queued to say how much of the gap is the weights.

How to serve a model without measuring your own harness. The first round of quality numbers here was wrong in a way that read like a finding: every model that reasons scored below every model that does not. Three bugs, each worth more than any kernel choice: no reasoning parser (vLLM registers one per family and we passed none, so the chain-of-thought was graded as the answer — 306 of 403 responses); a token cap that scored truncation as wrong (GLM lost 51% of its maths items that way); and greedy or T=0.6 sampling where every vendor specifies something else, with top_k and min_p needing extra_body to apply at all, and thinking off by default on Hy3 and gemma-4 but on for Qwen, Inkling, MiniMax, Nemotron, Ling and Ornith. The researched recipe per model lives in box/lists/profiles.tsv and is applied to both the server and the eval client.

7 · Selling idle capacity on Vast

Live market data from 86 RTX PRO 6000 listings, 3 September 2026: renters pay a median $1.51 per GPU-hour, hosts receive about $1.13 after commission, against a lease cost of $0.77; 74% of the fleet is rented. Subletting plausibly covers 75–100% of the lease. Interruptible listings clear lower, and hosting needs Docker, root, a public IP and an inbound port range. Vast currently lists zero RTX PRO 6000 nodes for rent, which is also why every experiment above ran serialised through one box.

8 · The question to ask before discussing price

Scan's material describes OpenVPN logins and vGPU-powered virtual workstations for its general cloud, and whole named machines for the RTX PRO 6000 line; neither mentions SSH, root or Docker. Three things would not survive a virtualised model: host BIOS control (PCIe ACS was worth 45–73% of interconnect bandwidth between two otherwise identical hosts), peer access between GPUs (vGPU commonly disables it; replicas are nearly immune, tensor-parallel is not), and public inbound networking (no public IP means no Vast hosting).

Five questions, before price. Is the 4× or 7× configuration dedicated bare metal with full PCIe passthrough, or vGPU on shared hardware? Do we get root on Linux with our own kernel modules, CUDA stack and Docker? Is PCIe ACS disabled, and will you run our NCCL test during the pilot? Do we get a public IP and a configurable inbound port range? Do your terms permit reselling idle capacity? If the first answer is vGPU, none of these throughput numbers transfer.

9 · Software maturity, priced honestly

  • The default FP8 block-scaled linear kernel crashes at dispatch on this architecture; an explicit backend override is required. The default NVFP4 kernel is a slow dequant path; another override is required.
  • DeepSeek's attention output projection needed a source patch we wrote ourselves; SGLang 0.5.18 cannot serve it here at all.
  • GLM-5.3-Flash needed two lifted vendor images, roughly a dozen patches between them, and community knowledge from the DGX Spark forums to produce correct output; the SGLang route still does not.
  • vLLM's platform check wrongly reports DeepGEMM support on these cards; the mixture-of-experts switch for it is separate from the one everybody sets; FlashInfer's autotuner deadlocks a TP group when only some ranks have a cached result.
  • Speculative decoding is lossless where it was tested properly — GLM's MTP head scores 0.872 against 0.864 on 367 paired items, 13 to 10 on the items only one side got right — but a first greedy-sequence test said the opposite, and only its control (the same server, twice: 4 of 12 identical) stopped that going out as an engine defect. Whether a drafter wins throughput at high concurrency is a per-shape question, recorded per arm with acceptance counters; the gittensor DSpark drafter does not load against its target at all.

10 · A second host, and what a 400 W power cap costs

At 16:45 on 3 September every rented instance was stopped at once. A stopped Vast instance keeps its disk but not its GPUs: within the hour another renter had the first box's four Server Edition cards, so its restart is queued indefinitely and its 1.1 TB of weights and results sit stranded (the results were already in the repository). The campaign moved to a replacement 4× RTX PRO 6000 host the same evening. It is the Workstation Edition, and the host caps each card at 400 W (the card allows 600). Under load all four sit at exactly 400.0 W with SM clocks near 1,290 MHz against a 3,090 MHz ceiling, and the driver reports the software power cap as the active limiter. 11 · Eight RTX 5090s against four RTX PRO 6000s, at the same monthly price

Scan sells an eight-way RTX 5090 machine, the GB8-32T, for the same £1,999.98 a month as the four-way RTX PRO 6000. That makes the comparison a straight read of intelligence and throughput per pound rather than per card. Eight consumer cards bring twice the tensor cores and a third of the memory each: 256 GB of aggregate memory against 384 GB, no card able to hold a model above about 30 GB on its own, and a third of the KV cache per server. The pattern is already legible. On short-prompt routing traffic, where the work is arithmetic and the cache is small, twice the silicon wins by roughly what you would expect. On shared-prefix prompt-optimisation traffic the advantage collapses to nothing, because 32 GB per card leaves an eighth of the cache and the scheduler starts to queue instead of batching. gpt-oss-20b shows the same shape from the other end: 25,634 output tokens a second at concurrency 1,024 on the 5090 node, and 39,529 on prompt-optimisation traffic, against 13,752 on the four-card node at its own best concurrency. The honest summary so far is that the consumer node is the better buy for high-volume short-context work and the professional node for anything memory-hungry, which is exactly the axis our own traffic spans. Two caveats belong with any use of these numbers: this rented 5090 host is dual-socket, so its four- and eight-way tensor-parallel tiers cross a socket boundary that Scan's single-socket machine does not have, and its cards are also capped at 400 W. The decision followed on 4 September: the 5090 machine is not suitable for this fleet. Nothing above about 30 GB fits one of its cards, so GLM-5.3-Flash and DeepSeek-V4-Flash need tensor parallelism across a socket boundary, and gpt-oss-120b at TP4 there ran at 1,640 output tokens a second with a 324 s time-to-first-token against 13,752 and 0.7 s as four replicas on the professional node. The 4× RTX PRO 6000 is the platform; everything since has been about getting the most out of it.
